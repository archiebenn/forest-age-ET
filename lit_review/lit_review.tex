\subsubsection{Evapotranspiration} 
Terrestrial evapotranspiration (ET) is a key component of the global hydrological cycle describing the transition of water from liquid to gas at Earth's surface and further transfer to the atmosphere, and is a crucial component of the energy and water exchange between terrestrial ecosystems and the climate system. The term emphasises the combination of water vapourisation flux from (1) abiotic evaporation from soil and plant canopy surfaces, and (2) biotic transpiration through stomata to the atmosphere \citep{katul2012evapotranspiration}. ET can be generally described through:

\begin{equation}
ET = T + E_{Soil} + E_{Canopy}
\label{eqn:et}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $T$ refers to transpiration through plant stomata, $E_{soil}$ to evaporation from the soil surface, and $E_{Canopy}$ to evaporation from plant canopies. The contribution of these variables to overall ET is not equal, with \cite{lian2018partitioning} estimating a global $T/ET$ ratio of 0.62$\pm$0.06, indicating plant transpiration as the leading component of ET worldwide. The partitioning of $ET$ into $E$ ($E_{soil} + E_{Canopy}$) and $T$ within ecosystems is associated with ``water use efficiency" and has use in irrigation management, where $T$ is linked with plant productivity, while $E$ does not directly contribute to production \citep{kool2014review}. This in turn likely explains the elevated $E/ET$ ratios in managed croplands compared to natural ecosystems \citep{yang2023evapotranspiration}. Accurate partitioning estimates are also important in aiding carbon cycle projections given the coupled effect of transpiration and land-atmosphere carbon dioxide exchanges through stomatal conductance.

\subsubsection{Canopy Cover and Vegetation Indices (CHANGE)}
ET is governed by many environmental factors such as solar radiation, leaf area index (LAI), air temperature, vapour pressure deficit (VPD), soil moisture, and wind speed \citep{yang2023evapotranspiration}. Of these, \cite{lian2018partitioning} identified LAI as the primary driver of $T/ET$ spatial patterns, and \cite{kool2014review} suggest that in systems with full canopy cover (and therefore high LAI) ET is often assumed to be similar enough to $T$ to permit correlation of ET with biomass. However, this assumption may break down in systems with multi-layer canopies such as forests despite high LAI. In these systems, the 2-dimensional measure of LAI will not capture the vertical canopy complexity which drives rainfall interception and subsequent $E_{Canopy}$, implying that canopy cover indices may struggle to explain $T/ET$ ratios in more complex vertical environments. This is reflected by \cite{zhou2016partitioning} who report higher coefficients of determination between enhanced vegetation index (EVI) and $T/ET$ in corn and soybean sites (0.84 and 0.82) with simple canopies, compared to evergreen needle leaf forests (0.37).
