\section{Discussion}
\subsection*{What this study addressed 100 words}
This study used ML models to form predictions of daily ET at FLUXNET forest sites across Europe and North America. While many previous studies have used FLUXNET with ML for ET predictions \parencite{}, using the age of a forest as a feature in these models has not been explored previously, and this study represents the first use of this feature for predictive ET modelling using ML methods. 

\subsection*{Key findings/results 250 words}
In all analyses contained within this study, site age contributed a meaningful impact on predictions of daily ET formed by RF models. The impact of using site age resulted in nuanced predictive accuracy changes, with large variation in the direction and magnitude of these performance shifts between sites in most analyses. 


\subsection*{Interpret results and compare with other papers/literature 600-700 words}
\subsubsection{Clustering}
\par Cluster analysis with PAM has been used successfully in hydro-ecology research previously \parencite{hou2025site, e2025multivariate}, but literature searches suggest that its use in ML ET predictive modelling is not common. Across cluster medoids in this study, model performance was highly diverse, with one suggestion for this being that training sites were pooled from a combination of sites across ecologically different clusters in all analyses. This may have led to under representation of specific cluster types in the training data and could explain why predictions at US-Me3, a geographically and characteristically distant site compared to others in the study (\autoref{fig:map} and \autoref{tab:cluster_characteristics}), were poor across all analyses - only 3 sites exist within the defined cluster, meaning that a maximum of 2 sites in the training pool could ever pass information into the RF models for predictions. \textcite{ferreira2022exploring} avoided this issue by training specific models which were trained and tested internally within each cluster. Clustering in \textcite{ferreira2022exploring} was also defined by variables which form the base of the FAO56-PM equation (used to calculate reference evapotranspiration $ET_0$ \parencite{allen2006recommendation}), ensuring that the resulting clusters are relevant to the ET estimations. While not formally verified, PAM clustering using Gower's distances in this study appeared to capture climate-relevant patterns, and could be of use for future research in ET generalisation modelling as a method to quantify regional diversity. 

\subsubsection{Model Sensitivity and Feature Selection}
Given the limitation of ML methods to extrapolate outside of feature values which have been `seen' during training \parencite{}, input feature selection has direct implications in model accessibility, interpretability, and predictive accuracy \parencite{liyew2025review}. Initial sensitivity testing at cluster medoids served two purposes. It provided direct evidence that feature selection impacts performance differently across sites, in agreement with the idea that no single, universally optimal feature set exists for ET predictions \parencite{liyew2025review}. This testing also established that the inclusion of forest age as a feature provided a measurable impact: the addition of site age was never neutral in terms of performance in any of the medoid test site predictions, providing initial evidence that site age can impact model decision structures. Models trained using easily acquired or measured variables should improve accessibility to ET predictions in data sparse regions, but predictive accuracy may be limited compared to models which are able to learn from a greater number of input sources, with \textcite{nayak2026estimation} demonstrating an $R^2$ improvement from 0.78 to 0.98 when moving from a data-limited to data rich DL models. The most predictable medoid sites in this study - US-UMd, DE-Lnf, and CA-Obs - typically followed an upward trajectory before plateauing with an increasing number of input features, and a strong level of predictive accuracy could be reached at these sites using only 2-3 input features, in agreement with similar findings in \textcite{nayak2026estimation}. Feature selection here was carried out using manual selection from variables understood to impact ET (\autoref{tab:features}). Feature sets were also constant across all analyses and models in this study which may have impacted performance inconsistently at different test sites. A better approach, as suggested in \textcite{liyew2025review}, may be to use a more flexible approach with systematic feature selection methods - which identify and select the best-performing input features and reduce unnecessary complexity during training - following initial manual selection from domain knowledge. \textcite{stapleton2022framework} used this method to enhance model performance and reduce dimensionality of training data for predictive ET modelling, critically without requiring advanced domain knowledge. Analyses with forest age added as a final feature to a model formed using this approach would allow more standardised assessments of ML performance across different training datasets by reducing user-selection bias.

\subsubsection{Model Generalisation}
\par Results from sampled training datasets in section \ref{sec:sampled} were highly variable in predictive performance. \textcite{ahmadi2025enhancing} formed models with the aim of enhance generalisation capacity and reduce overfitting predictions to a single site by training on diverse time series data from multiple sites in a manner similar to the present study. However, model performance from \textcite{ahmadi2025enhancing} was far stronger and more stable across unseen sites compared to the present study. This is likely because, as in \textcite{ferreira2022exploring}, training and testing sites were confined to much smaller and similar geographic regions; all 55 sites from \textcite{ahmadi2025enhancing} were contained within the Central Valley of California - a climatically homogeneous region. In turn, training and test data were likely confined within a much narrower range compared to this study which should enable higher predictive accuracy, although this is also confounded with the use of deep learning and automatic hyperparameter optimisation per model in \textcite{ahmadi2025enhancing} which was not utilised here. This claim is partially supported by regression analysis in section \ref{sec:sampled}, where overall site similarity to the test site (using Gower's distance) was able to predict $R^2$ in both models across the full set of medoid sites, with accuracy falling as similarity reduced. This relationship was also significant at the finer site-level scale for 4/6 medoid test sites, indicating that the all site trend was not just an artifact from cross-site pooling.
\subsubsection{Forest Site Age as a Feature}
This study was positioned by previous work carried out in \textcite{besnard2018} where forest age was used as a predictor in net ecosystem productivity (NEP) estimations using RF models. While current literature surrounding the use of forest age in ML ET models is sparse, this prior work was able to establish forest age as a dominant factor in NEP statio-temporal variability at a global scale, providing evidence that models are able to learn from forest age for flux-predictions using RF. 
\par The present study found more nuanced and site-specific results while investigating ET predictions, despite using 50 FLUXNET forest sites which were also used and dated in the NEP study. For feature selection, \textcite{besnard2018} carried out a similar method (reversed) with features sequentially removed, and found that removing forest age always substantially decreased model performance, contrasting to the current study in which forest age appeared to have a less substantial impact. There are many possible explanations for the contrasting impact observed on the two fluxes in both of these studies. Crucially, \textcite{besnard2018} predicted carbon fluxes rather than water fluxes. Carbon fluxes such as NEP across forest ecosystems may be more directly correlated with the age of those forests than any ET-age relationship, encouraging the RF models to place greater importance on forest age when forming NEP predictions. Furthermore, `age' in the models used in \textcite{besnard2018} was not solely raw age as in the present study. Instead, a pre-defined nonlinear relationship between NEP and age \parencite{amiro2010ecosystem} was fitted, and a predicted value per site year from this was treated as a separate variable in the models alongside raw age. In contrast, the present study was designed to let the RF models draw any ET-age relationships from raw age alone. \textcite{oishi2020} demonstrated an age-ET relationship but this was particular to a specific region and species composition; no generalisable equation form exists as with NEP in \parencite{amiro2010ecosystem}. Even if one did exist, forcing it as a fixed input into a model, as in \textcite{besnard2018}, risks imposing model outputs which are constrained to a specific pattern and may not hold true across all sites. The use of raw age alone in this study likely led to a noisier age-target relationship across models, and could explain the highly site-dependent impacts on predictions compared to the clean global signal in \textcite{besnard2018}, independent of whether the target was NEP or ET.

\subsubsection{Case Study}
\par %how model age genuinely does impact predictions, not just as a proxy for other site level features (shuffle and also delta et bits)
US-UMd and DE-Lnf were highly predictable sites but had opposing reactions in predictive performance with the addition of age. Overall SHAP model importance at both sites placed shortwave radiation, within-year temporal representation value, LAI, and air temperature as the most important variables when forming predictions at both sites. Air temperature, shortwave radiation, and LAI are commonly ranked highly in ET predictive model feature importance \parencite{liyew2025review}. However, within-year temporal representations, such as \texttt{cos\_DOY} which was ranked alongside shortwave radiation here, do not appear as highly ranked in other studies. This could reflect a limitation of using a fixed calendar encoding method: \texttt{cos\_DOY} is fixed spatially while ET patterns are not, which could impact model generalisation across large latitudes. A better approach may be to include a variable for the hour angle at sunset as in \textcite{huang2024integrating}, which would not only capture time throughout the year at sites but may additionally capture plant stomatal open and closure timings which is crucial in transpiration rates \parencite{resco2016genetic}, potentially aiding generalisation across larger latitudes. 
\par `Mod' and `Mod + Age' predictions at both sites suggested that summer extremes tended to be under-predicted and winter lows were typically over-predicted at both sites (\autoref{fig:scatters}). Comparing SHAP values for site age in the `Mod + Age' models highlighted a potential reason behind opposing performance metrics: DE-Lnf prediction values typically increased by the inclusion of site age compared to the baseline (positive SHAP), and decreasing compared to the baseline (negative SHAP) at US-UMd (\autoref{fig:shap_preds}). One hypothesis was explored further: the inclusion of site age was impacting predictions during already over-predicted periods (typically winters). Temporal plots of $\Delta$Predicted ET (`Mod + Age' - `Mod' per prediction) against time alongside a SHAP heatmap supported this idea. Site age SHAP feature importance fluctuated seasonally (\autoref{fig:diffs_et}), with the greatest absolute values appearing to line up with winter at both sites and lowest absolute values lining up with summer. Crucially, $\Delta$Predicted ET followed a similar pattern. These $\Delta$Predicted ET values are caused by the inclusion of site age, but may not necessarily be driven by the variable \texttt{Site\_age} itself as RF models can adapt how decisions are split across all features with the addition of a single new feature, so changes in predictions may be indirectly caused by features already included in `Mod' \parencite{}. Formal testing was therefore required to confirm \texttt{Site\_age} was driving these changes. This was supported by regression analysis of $\Delta$Predicted ET against SHAP values for \texttt{Site\_age}, \texttt{SW\_rad}, and \texttt{cos\_DOY}, which found \texttt{Site\_age} to be highly significant and by far the strongest predictor at both sites (\autoref{tab:mlr_umd} and \autoref{tab:mlr_lnf}). This strongly supports site age as the driver of performance shifts, with this influence likely following a seasonal pattern: `Mod + Age' winter ET predictions increased at DE-Lnf away from observed values, reducing overall $R^2$, but decreased at US-UMd towards observed values, increasing overall $R^2$. 
% are these issues/benefits with site age coming during winter months when the model has less variable data in other predictors to rely on, but the site age is constant and so the additive/drop of each becomes more obvious?
As summer predictions were typically under-predicted in `Mod', similar shifts (down at US-UMd, up at DE-Lnf) from site age SHAP could have offset the overall $R^2$ changes. However, as absolute SHAP were lowest in summer, this counteracting effect was minimal and the overall $R^2$ change between models can be attributed to these shifts in winter months at both sites. 
\par The exact mechanism for why forest age included as a feature drives predictive performance in opposite directions at these two sites remains unclear from this analysis, and the inherent lack of explainability in ML models makes this difficult to determine with confidence  \parencite{hassija2024interpreting}.  Forest age varies substantially between sites but little within each, unlike other dynamic predictors in the models. The RF models may be exploiting by using age less as a daily predictor and more as a site-level offset for ET: a metric to direct predictions in a constant direction based on age relative to the training set. A SHAP dependence plot for site age supports this hypothesis: an almost stepwise response in SHAP importance for \texttt{Site\_age} is visible around the all-site mean forest age (\autoref{fig:shap_dependence}). This implies the models may be learning an age-ET relationship where ET is greater at older sites, with US-UMd and DE-Lnf falling on opposite sides of the mean explaining their divergent responses and leading to the overall $R^2$ changes with this importance varies seasonally. Removing any forest age-ET relationship by shuffling ages between sites before carrying out a train-test cycle resulted in an $R^2$ performance shift back towards `Mod' levels at both sites, despite the presence of a site age feature (\autoref{fig:case_study_bars}). This is suggestive evidence that models were relying on some relationship between real forest age and ET which predictive decisions are being split on: if forest age was being used simply as a proxy for other input features, performance should have been more similar in the shuffled and `Mod + Age' forms and not the shuffled and `Mod' forms. If an age-ET relationship is the causal mechanism of these predictive differences, the diverging $R^2$ outcomes at each site may be an artifact of season-specific importance of site age as a feature: influence on predictions is strongest in winter, and depending on the position of each site in the age-ET relationship, this can push winter predictions in a direction that either improves (US-UMd) or worsens (DE-Lnf) overall $R^2$, with the weaker importance during summer months unable to counteract this effect. 
\par \autoref{fig:age_et_all} shows the overall sum of ET and 90 day peak ET sum against forest age for site years at all sites in the study, and a nonlinear correlation between ET and forest age is visible in both cases. This follows a similar overall shape as the SHAP dependence plot and could represent an age-ET relationship being learned by the models.

Why this relationship exists at the sites in this study is difficult to interpret. The present study contains various tree species in different climate zones at a small number of sites, and therefore it is unlikely for a clear age-ET relationship to emerge which fits all sites, resulting in models which then try to base predictions on a noisy relationship. Forests of different species and climates will undergo inconsistent developmental dynamics \parencite{} with impacts on ET rates - for example, stomatal conductance directly impacts transpiration and is species-specific \parencite{}, and canopy density can likewise affect evaporation \parencite{}. This means that the formation of a model to test the effects of forest age on ET should be trained on sites belonging to similar species and climate as the test sites.

Here lies the main limitation of this study - such a heterogeneous training set makes learning relationships difficult to learn for ML models, even if these relationships may be true under certain conditions. This ties back to the highly varied predictive accuracy across different clusters (\autoref{fig:r2_gower}) - ultimately, underrepresented sites in the feature space during training were harder to predict, a phenomenon in ML known AS X? \parencite{}. Uncertainty in the initial determination of site ages by \textcite{besnard2018}, FLUXNET gap-filling and accuracy issues \parencite{hill2017case}, and the use of an inflexible set of variables and parameters during RF training (section \ref{sec:RF}) likely did not help with overall predictive accuracy and uncertainty, but the main source of error here falls back to the high heterogeneity in training dataset composition.  While an age-ET relationship was learned here by the models and demonstrated impacts on predictions with the inclusion of age as a feature, if this underlying relationship is messy and confounded by different factors due to highly varied training data, then the impacts on predictions at unseen sites will also be noisy and not necessarily reflective of a true biophysical association, as was seen here. This necessitates reflecting on the reliance of $R^2$ as a measure for a `good' model. $R^2$ improved at US-UMd but worsened at DE-Lnf with `Mod + Age', but this appeared to only be as a result of season-specific predictions either driving ET estimates up or down, itself likely as a result of where each site age sits in the age-ET space of the training data. Neither model is necessarily better or worse in terms of capturing ET dynamics with respect to forest age if the aim is to produce a model which generalises well to unseen sites. 

\subsection*{Implications and directions on/for future research 250 words}
% hybrid modelling - form target ET from defined ewuations
% species-specific modelling may help age estimates 
Developing ML models specific to a tree species or cover type of interest should reduce confounding factors in terms of vegetation dynamics and aid models in learning age-ET relationships if they exist. The availability of age-dated sites is limited, and this study was constrained in the ability to form species-specific models given the number of age-dated forest sites in the FLUXNET. A potential avenue to circumvent this issue would be the use of hybrid ML modelling, in which physics-based methods are paired with ML models - an approach \textcite{liyew2025review} state is under-explored but shows promising results in ET predictive modelling.  \textcite{jiang2023hybrid} used this framework to simulate ET values using physics-based methods in a data-deficient basin, before training RF models with these values as a target. Applying this approach may increase prediction uncertainty as the target variable is not directly measured, but it could significantly aid the spatial expansion of training data. If this approach could be combined with accurate measurements of forest ages - either from field-based surveys or with novel pipelines to identify forest turnover events and species identification with LiDAR \parencite{fischer2025global} - enough training data may be available to permit the formation of species-specific models for ET predictions. This represents the next step in investigating forest age in ET prediction: species-specific models would learn cleaner age-ET relationships than a pooled training set of mixed species, providing a basis for further investigation into predictive performance at single-species forest sites. Future work here is suggested and could reveal whether site age is appropriate for use on a species-by-species basis.
% zooming out: age as an explainer vs explained to close discussion and implications to why et predictions are actualy important
\par One further suggested line of investigation, and one which would determine the usefulness of forest age in ET prediction, lies in whether age represents an `explainer' or `explained' variable. The basis of the present study was to identify if forest age may indirectly capture properties of forests which might relate to ET dynamics over their lifetime, providing a variable which could be useful in forming more accurate predictions of ET. If other measurements are able to capture these forest properties relating to ET dynamics directly however, using age as a proxy for these forest properties becomes redundant as a feature in modelling, particularly if these other measurements are easily acquired and spatially abundant, and age can be thought of as `explained'. For instance, rather than using forest age as an indirect measure of canopy density and thus ET, directly measuring this with LAI or NDVI might already provide all necessary information for ML models to learn how ET changes with density, bypassing the use of age entirely. Equally, if forest age is encoding information which cannot be explained by other measurements, or if it able to capture the information encoded within multiple measurements and provides a more simple index to be used in models, age can be thought of as an `explainer', and using it as a feature would be beneficial. Determining which category forest age sits in with respect to ET dynamics should direct the necessity for further work.
