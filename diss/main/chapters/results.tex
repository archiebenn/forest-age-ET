\section{Results}

\subsection{Flux Tower Site Clustering} \label{sec:cluster_res}
Cluster analysis was carried out in order to classify sites into groups based on their characteristics. This classification permits the predictability of \ac{ET}, both within these characterised groups and across them, to be investigated. Gower's distances, a measure between pairs of data points, from 0 = completely dissimilar to 1 = completely similar \parencite{gower1971} (\ref{sec:cluster}), were computed using the features listed in \autoref{tab:cluster_vars} and saved as a matrix. This matrix was passed to the \ac{PAM} algorithm \parencite{rousseeuw1990} with the silhouette method to form clusters and identify medoid sites - those which have minimal dissimilarity to others within each cluster and thus can be seen as the representative sites of each cluster \parencite{schubert2021fast}.
\par The assignment solution from \ac{PAM} with $k = 6$ clusters can be seen in a cluster plot of the first two principal components (\autoref{fig:cluster}). The geographic distribution of clusters appears to follow an expected pattern with regards to climatic groups; for example, sites at high latitudes tended to be grouped in cluster 3, with Mediterranean and coastal sites typically grouped in cluster 5 (\autoref{fig:map}). This expected pattern of clusters suggests that the \ac{PAM} clustering solution captures climate-relevant patterns to some degree, although this was not formally tested. The six medoid sites were geographically distributed with four sites in North America and two sites in Europe (\autoref{fig:map}), and a range of site ages from 20 years (US-Me3) to 117 years (DE-Lnf). 
\par Site characteristics $\geq$ 1 sd from the all site mean were considered as `high/low' ($\geq$ 1.5 sd as `very high/very low') in order to qualitatively classify cluster characteristics, visualised in (\autoref{fig:pheat}) and summarised in \autoref{tab:cluster_characteristics}.

\subsection{Model Sensitivity} \label{sec:model_sens}
Results from model sensitivity testing indicate mixed model performance across the medoid sites, based on \ac{R2}, \ac{RMSE}, and \ac{MAE} between the predicted and observed daily \ac{ET} (\autoref{fig:adding_features}). Here, features were sequentially added to \ac{RF} models, and these were re-trained with each additional feature before testing on all unseen medoid sites (\ref{sec:sens}).
\par Predictions of daily \ac{ET} formed for CA-Obs, US-UMd, and DE-Lnf showed strong performance across the range of input features, while CA-TP3, IT-SRo, and US-Me3 was less predictable by the models overall. 
\par CA-Obs and DE-Lnf showed very similar patterns of performance, with predictions at both sites typically strong and climbing across metrics up until \texttt{cos\_DOY} was added (Peak CA-Obs: $R^2$ = 0.90; Peak DE-Lnf: $R^2$ = 0.90). Predictions at both sites then declined with the inclusion of forest age to the model ($\Delta R^2$ with \texttt{Site\_age}: CA-Obs = -0.07; DE-Lnf = -0.04). US-UMd deviated from this pattern, with performance improving further when \texttt{Site\_age} was included in the model (Peak US-UMd: $R^2$ = 0.90, $\Delta$$R^2$ with \texttt{Site\_age} = +0.03). At all three of these sites, using only a single feature in the \ac{RF} model, \texttt{T\_air}, demonstrated strong to moderate predictive performance (CA-Obs: $R^2$ = 0.76; US-UMd: $R^2$ = 0.64; DE-Lnf: $R^2$ = 0.63).
\par Overall predictive performance at IT-SRo was poor (Peak IT-SRo: $R^2$ = 0.28). Performance at the remaining two sites, CA-TP3 and US-Me3, was particularly mixed and relatively poor (Peak CA-TP3: $R^2$ = 0.55; Peak US-Me3: $R^2$ = 0.00). $R^2$ fluctuated between predictions made with new features at these two sites, with performance at US-Me3 indicating that the model has no explanatory power at this site from a peak $R^2$ of 0.0. 
\par Ratios of \ac{RMSE}:\ac{MAE} from peak predictive models at each medoid all fall between 1.33 and 1.48, suggesting that the impact of outlier \ac{ET} predictions, and overall error distribution, was largely consistent from the best performing models at each site during this analysis. 


\subsection{Train-Test Distance Analysis Using Sampled Training} \label{sec:sampled}
\autoref{fig:paired_box} shows the paired results between the `Mod' and the `Mod + Age' models (described by \autoref{eqn:mod} and \autoref{eqn:mod_age} respectively) during the shuffled and sampled training set analysis (\ref{sec:shuffle}). Including age had a significant impact across a range of training set compositions: all six medoid sites displayed significant differences in $R^2$ between `Mod' and `Mod + Age' predictions for daily \ac{ET} (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, $p < 0.01$ for all).
\par Mean Gower's and age distances between training set and test sites were calculated for each of the 100 runs to assess how training set composition influenced predictive performance (\ref{sec:shuffle}). Pooled site results follow an expected pattern: predictive accuracy falls as ecological and age dissimilarity increases between the training data and test sites in both models (\autoref{fig:r2_gower}). For the `Mod' model, the multiple linear regression was statistically significant ($R^2 = 0.60, F(2, 597) = 453.8, p < 0.001$), and both predictors independently explained $R^2_{Mod}$ (Gower's: $\beta = -6.19, p < 0.001$; Age: $\beta = -0.0064, p < 0.001$). The `Mod + Age' model was also significant ($R^2 = 0.54, F(2, 597) = 349, p < 0.001$), and both variables independently explaining $R^2_{Mod + Age}$ (Gower's: $\beta = -7.03, p < 0.001$; Age: $\beta = -0.0056, p < 0.001$). As site age was included as a variable during cluster analysis (\autoref{tab:cluster_vars}), mean Gower's and age distances are not fully independent. However, very low multicollinearity ($VIF = 1.52$ for both predictors in each model) suggests each predictor contributes distinct information.  
\par This similarity-performance relationship holds with all sites pooled, but is not present within each individual site. Simple linear models within sites suggest Gower's distance showed no significant relationship with $R^2$ at CA-TP3 in either model, and was significantly positively associated with $R^2$ in `Mod + Age' predictions at US-Me3. Similarly, mean age distance was only significantly associated with $R^2$ at US-UMd for both models, with no significant relationship present at the other five medoid sites in either model.

\subsection{Case Study of US-UMd and DE-Lnf}
US-UMd and DE-Lnf share similar site qualities (\autoref{tab:tab:site_summary}) and both show high predictability for daily \ac{ET} using ML models, yet the predictive performance responses in both section \ref{sec:model_sens} and section \ref{sec:sampled} were opposite when age was added as a feature. Further analyses focused on these two sites (\ref{sec:case_study}).

\subsubsection{Observed vs. Predicted}\label{sec:obs}
\par \autoref{subfig:umd1} and \autoref{subfig:lnf1} display scatter plots comparing the predicted and observed daily \ac{ET} from each model at the sites. At DE-Lnf, the addition of age appears to cause low observed \ac{ET} to be over-predicted, particularly in winter and autumn, in an opposite response to predictions of low observed \ac{ET} at US-UMd. All models appear to under-predict peak daily \ac{ET} in summer. Performance metrics followed the same pattern as in \ref{sec:model_sens} and \ref{sec:sampled}, with $R^2$ improving in US-UMd predictions ($R^2_{Mod} = 0.86$; $R^2_{Mod + Age} = 0.90$) and dropping in DE-Lnf predictions ($R^2_{Mod} = 0.90$; $R^2_{Mod + Age} = 0.86$) with the addition of forest age as a feature. 

\subsubsection{Time Series Plots and SHAP Feature Importance} \label{sec:obs2}
\par Time series plots of predictions from `Mod + Age' (\autoref{subfig:umd2} and \autoref{subfig:lnf2}) indicate strong cyclical predictive performance overall at both sites. Both models captured abnormal lows during summer months; for example, an \ac{ET} rate which is typically seen during mid-winter was largely captured at US-UMd in summer 2011, demonstrating flexibility in these \ac{RF} models. Despite this, extreme \ac{ET} peaks were missed in consecutive years at US-UMd. For instance, a peak in summer 2010 was under-predicted by the model by nearly 2mm/day. Winters appeared to be problematic for the models, with \ac{ET} estimations being consistently over-predicted and troughs missed in all years at both sites. Mean \ac{SHAP} importance order was similar at both sites, with \texttt{SW\_rad} and \texttt{cos\_DOY} dominating predictions in both models, followed by \texttt{T\_air} and \texttt{LAI}. Site age was ranked as the 6th most important predictor at US-UMd and 5th at DE-Lnf. Mean \ac{SHAP} values also indicate that site age had opposite impacts on predictions at both sites: age typically reduced predictions at US-UMd and increased them at DE-Lnf. 

\subsubsection{SHAP heatmap and $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET}} \label{case:3}
\par To assess how \ac{SHAP} values varied over time in the `Mod + Age' model, mean weekly \ac{SHAP} importance for features was plotted as a time-series heatmap, presented alongside a graph representing how \ac{ET} predictions changed ($\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET}) between the `Mod' and `Mod + Age' models at both sites in \autoref{fig:diffs_et} (\ref{sec:case_study}). Heatmaps suggest clear, yearly cyclical patterns of \ac{SHAP} importance for all features apart from \texttt{P\_sum\_14D} and \texttt{W\_speed}. Heatmap \ac{SHAP} values for \texttt{Site\_age} appear to line up with the $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET} plot shifts from the $y = 0$ baseline for both sites. For example, $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET} drops from the baseline most years at US-UMd appear to line up consistently with lower \ac{SHAP} values for \texttt{Site\_age} (\autoref{subfig:diff_umd}). Similarly, at DE-Lnf, high peaks above the baseline correspond with high \ac{SHAP} values for \texttt{Site\_age}, such as in late 2007, mid 2010, and early 2012 (\autoref{subfig:diff_lnf}).
\par Scatter plots of $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET} against \texttt{Site\_age} \ac{SHAP} values for the sites (\autoref{fig:umd_scatter} and \autoref{fig:lnf_scatter}) both indicate a strong, near linear relationship. Multiple linear regressions using \texttt{SW\_rad}, \texttt{cos\_DOY}, and \texttt{Site\_age} \ac{SHAP} values as predictors confirmed that \texttt{Site\_age} \ac{SHAP} was the dominant predictor of $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET} at both sites (US-UMd: $\beta = 2.47, SE = 0.049, p < 0.01$; DE-Lnf: $\beta = 2.49, SE = 0.036, p < 0.01$). In contrast, \texttt{SW\_rad} and \texttt{cos\_DOY} \ac{SHAP} values were minor, but significant predictors of $\Delta$Predicted \ac{ET} in all but \texttt{SW\_rad} at US-UMd, which was not significant (full outputs in \autoref{tab:mlr_umd} and \autoref{tab:mlr_lnf}). This strongly suggests that prediction differences between `Mod' and `Mod + Age' were shaped by how \texttt{Site\_age} is used within the \ac{RF} models, rather than by the addition of this predictor altering decision splits in other features. 

\subsubsection{Manipulating Training Ages} \label{case:4}
Three new models were formed to investigate if real age-\ac{ET} relationships were being learned by the models: (1) `Mod' (2) `Mod + Age' (3) `Mod + Shuffled Age', where training site ages were randomly shuffled site-wise (\ref{sec:case_study}).
\par Consistent with previous findings, daily \ac{ET} predictive performance increased with the addition of age as a feature at US-UMd ($R^2_{Mod} = 0.86 \pm 0.00$; $R^2_{Mod + Age} = 0.90 \pm 0.00$), and decreased at DE-Lnf ($R^2_{Mod} = 0.90\pm 0.00$; $R^2_{Mod + Age} = 0.86\pm 0.00$) in models formed with all five \texttt{NumPy} seeds (\autoref{fig:case_study_bars}). At both sites, the shuffled age model moved predictive performance back towards the `Mod' model (US-UMd $R^2_{Shuffled} = 0.85 \pm 0.03$; DE-Lnf $R^2_{Shuffled} = 0.89 \pm 0.02$). 


\subsection{Results Summary}
Site age as a feature in \ac{RF} models caused site-specific and non-uniform effects on daily \ac{ET} predictions in all analyses. Train-test similarity analyses demonstrated that predictive accuracy declined as ecological and age dissimilarity increased, although these relationships were not as robust when assessed within individual sites. A case study of two sites identified that including age improved predictive performance at US-UMd but reduced it at DE-Lnf, despite both sites demonstrating high baseline predictability. Time-series \ac{SHAP} analysis revealed a temporally consistent impact on \ac{ET} predictions from site age at both sites, and modelling \ac{SHAP} values against the change in \ac{ET} predictions between `Mod' and `Mod + Age' strongly suggested site age was the dominant predictor driving these differences. Shuffling ages between sites during training caused predictive accuracy to shift from `Mod + Age' back towards `Mod' levels at both sites, despite age being present as a predictor. Together, these results show that the effect of site age as a feature in \ac{RF} models is nuanced, site-specific, and detectable across numerous modelling approaches for predictions of daily \ac{ET}.
